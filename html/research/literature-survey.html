Literature Survey

Grounding, verification and multi-agent hallucination research · compiled 2026-08-13 · background research agent, model sonnet
Four questions, asked to find out whether Groundswarm's core bet (a fail-closed verifier that independently re-checks a swarm's combined output against live primary sources) is validated, contested, or simply unbuilt in the existing literature. Short answer: the individual pieces are well studied, but nobody has put them together at the swarm level, and the literature itself flags "fail closed" as a genuinely unsolved problem, not a solved recipe to import.

1. Hallucination detection, retrieval-grounding, citation verification

Established, actively evolving
RAG-as-grounding is mature; the frontier has moved to checking what RAG produced. Consistency-based black-box detection is anchored by SelfCheckGPT and semantic entropy (Nature, 2024). Atomic-fact scoring comes from FActScore, tool-augmented re-verification from FacTool. For citations specifically, ALCE (EMNLP 2023) is the standard benchmark, and RARR retrofits citations and revises unsupported claims after the fact.

Cheap, high-fidelity grounding checks are now practical: MiniCheck matches GPT-4-level fact-checking at roughly 400x lower cost, a plausible building block for a Groundswarm verifier rather than something that has to be invented from scratch.

Most directly relevant to Groundswarm's framing: Cited but Not Verified: Source Attribution in LLM Deep Research Agents and Detecting and Correcting Reference Hallucinations in Commercial LLMs and Deep Research Agents both find commercial "deep research" agents routinely emit citations that don't actually support the claim, even single-vendor systems that already advertise grounding. Caution flag: The Illusion of Progress: Re-evaluating Hallucination Detection (EMNLP 2025) argues current hallucination-detection evals themselves overstate how much progress has been made, worth remembering before trusting any single metric.

2. LLM-as-judge: strengths, failure modes, fail-closed design

Well documented fail-closed still immature
Zheng et al., Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena (NeurIPS 2023) is foundational: strong judges reach roughly 80% human agreement, but the paper itself names position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias as open problems. Self-preference is now causally demonstrated, not just observed: LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations (NeurIPS 2024) shows self-recognition capability correlates linearly with self-preference strength, meaning the judge model matters mechanically, not just as a config choice.

Rubric-gaming is an active 2025–2026 subfield: RIFT taxonomizes rubric failure modes, Rulers proposes locked, evidence-anchored rubrics against instability, Autorubric separates criteria into independent calls specifically to kill halo effects. This is the same shape of problem the capstone's 41-check rubric was built to resist.

Most load-bearing for the "fails closed" goal: AbstentionBench finds abstention is unsolved and does not improve with model scale, reasoning models still guess when they should decline, and The Judge Knows When It Knows argues a few high-precision calls plus honest abstention beats confident wrong answers everywhere. That is Groundswarm's exact design bet, and the literature confirms it is a real engineering problem to solve, not a checkbox to enable.

3. Multi-agent error compounding and verifying swarm output

This is where Groundswarm's novelty actually sits
Cemri et al., Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail? (MAST) analyzed 1,600+ traces across 7 frameworks and found 14 failure modes across 3 categories, system design, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification, with high inter-rater agreement (κ=0.88). A meaningful share of real multi-agent failures are missing or incomplete verification, not raw single-model hallucination, which is measured evidence the problem Groundswarm targets is real in production systems today.

Most directly relevant: Hallucination Cascade: Error Propagation in Multi-Agent LLM Systems (Polytechnique Montréal) tracks atomic claims through agent chains and finds a counterintuitive result: the normalized hallucination score drops across a 3-agent chain (0.422→0.272, later agents smooth earlier errors' surface texture) while ground-truth factual accuracy actually declines (0.789→0.769), and 7.3% of hops actively amplify errors. Swarms can look more coherent while becoming less true, which is precisely the failure mode a verifier needs to catch rather than a hypothetical risk.

GSAR ("typed grounding") is the closest architectural analog found, checkpointing peer-agent outputs before downstream integration, worth reading as convergent prior art rather than a competing product. Existing multi-agent fact-checkers (FC-MAD, MERMAID) verify one claim via internal debate, not the combined output of an upstream production swarm, which is the gap Groundswarm's verifier is aimed at.

4. Benchmarks for verified-vs-plausible multi-agent output

Clearest open gap found
No benchmark specifically scores "genuinely grounded vs. merely plausible" for multi-agent systems. Adjacent pieces exist, GAIA (live-web, exact-match ground truth), LLM-Coordination (coordination ability, not factuality), the MAST failure taxonomy (categorizes failures, doesn't score them), but nothing composes them into a single eval.

This is genuinely unbuilt: an evaluation asking whether combining N agents increases or decreases groundedness versus a single agent, checkable by an independent re-fetcher. That gap is exactly what would need to be built to validate Groundswarm's bet as research, separately from validating it as a product.

What this means for Groundswarm

Check 1: the individual techniques the verifier needs (grounding checks, citation round-tripping, LLM-as-judge) are mature enough to build on, several with cheap off-the-shelf implementations like MiniCheck.
Check 2: "fail closed" is confirmed hard, not solved, by AbstentionBench, so the deterministic re-fetch layer the capstone already proved matters more than the LLM judge, the judge is a secondary check, not the primary guarantee.
Check 3: swarm-level verification of combined, multi-agent output is a real, named, and apparently unaddressed gap in both the academic literature and (per the market survey) the commercial tooling landscape, which is the strongest evidence found that this is worth building.
